02Target architecture

What it needs to become

The current design puts every business rule in the browser. Pricing is resolved in React, seat counts are written from React, and there is nothing to stop a determined person writing whatever they like. The fix is to move the rules behind the database.

Layer 01

Postgres holds the truth

Fare bands, seat availability, invoice totals and document numbers become constraints, generated columns and exclusion indexes — not conventions. If a rule matters, the database refuses to break it, regardless of what calls it.

Layer 02

Row-level security, from day one

Staff roles see the back office. A customer sees their own account and nothing else. A subcontracted operator sees their own vehicles, drivers and trips — never your customer list or your margins.

Layer 03

Scheduled jobs do the repetitive work

Edge functions on a schedule: materialise next month's trips from the timetable, raise the billing run, expire stale seat holds, chase overdue accounts, flag licences and inspections before they lapse.

Layer 04

Two front ends, one schema

The back office you already have, repaired and routed properly. Plus a thin customer portal for self-service sign-up, payment and manage-my-seat — the thing that stops your team being the only interface to the business.

03The fare model

Many prices on one route

This is the centre of what you asked for, and the thing the current schema cannot do at all.

Why it does not work today

The entire pricing model is four columns:

route_pricing (route_id, weekly_price, monthly_price, effective_from, effective_to)
subscriptions (…, price_override numeric)

That is exactly one weekly price and one monthly price per route, per date window. Two prices cannot be live on the same route at the same time. So when your prices range, the only place that variation can go is price_override — a bare number typed onto each subscription, attached to no named plan.

What that costs you in practice

You cannot answer "how many riders are on the R1,650 rate?" You cannot raise prices for new customers while protecting existing ones. You cannot see that a staff member sold a seat R400 under the floor. And when you change route_pricing, every historical invoice silently re-prices, because the price was never stored on the subscription — only resolved from the route at read time.

The model that replaces it

Three ideas, in order of importance.

Idea 01

A route sells many named plans

A fare plan is a sellable product on a route: a code, a name, a billing period, an audience. A route can have as many as you like, all live at once.

Idea 02

Each plan is a band, not a number

Every plan carries a floor, a list and a ceiling. That is how "prices range" becomes a rule the system enforces instead of a habit staff carry in their heads.

Idea 03

The agreed price locks to the subscription

What the customer agreed is written onto their subscription and never recomputed. Raising the list price affects new sales only, until you deliberately re-rate.

The schema

-- A named, sellable price point on a route.
-- One route can carry as many of these as you need, all active at once.
create table fare_plans (
  fare_plan_id    bigint generated always as identity primary key,
  route_id        bigint  not null references routes(route_id) on delete restrict,
  code            text    not null,   -- 'SOW-SAN-MTH-5D'
  name            text    not null,   -- 'Monthly, 5 days a week'

  billing_period  text    not null check (billing_period in
                    ('weekly','monthly','termly','once_off')),
  audience        text    not null default 'individual' check (audience in
                    ('individual','corporate','scholar','staff')),

  -- the dimensions your prices actually vary on
  days_per_week   smallint check (days_per_week between 1 and 7),
  direction       text    check (direction in ('one_way','return')),
  boarding_zone   text,                    -- board closer in, pay less
  min_seats       smallint not null default 1,   -- bulk tiers for companies

  is_active       boolean  not null default true,
  created_at      timestamptz not null default now(),

  unique (route_id, code)
);

-- Prices are versioned. History is never overwritten, so an invoice
-- raised in March can always be explained in September.
create table fare_plan_versions (
  version_id      bigint generated always as identity primary key,
  fare_plan_id    bigint not null references fare_plans(fare_plan_id) on delete cascade,

  list_price      numeric(10,2) not null check (list_price    >= 0),
  floor_price     numeric(10,2) not null check (floor_price   >= 0),
  ceiling_price   numeric(10,2) not null check (ceiling_price >= 0),
  currency        char(3) not null default 'ZAR',

  effective_from  date not null,
  effective_to    date,

  check (floor_price <= list_price and list_price <= ceiling_price),
  check (effective_to is null or effective_to > effective_from),

  -- the database itself refuses two overlapping prices on one plan
  exclude using gist (
    fare_plan_id with =,
    daterange(effective_from, coalesce(effective_to, 'infinity'::date), '[)') with &&
  )
);

-- The subscription binds to a plan and locks the agreed price.
alter table subscriptions
  add column fare_plan_id     bigint references fare_plans(fare_plan_id),
  add column agreed_price     numeric(10,2),   -- per seat, per period
  add column price_locked_at  timestamptz,
  add column discount_reason  text;            -- required if below list

A trigger validates agreed_price against the plan's live band at the moment of sale, and demands a reason for anything below list. That single rule is what turns a range from a liability into a policy.

create function enforce_fare_band() returns trigger as $$
declare v fare_plan_versions%rowtype;
begin
  select * into v from fare_plan_versions
   where fare_plan_id = new.fare_plan_id
     and daterange(effective_from, coalesce(effective_to,'infinity'::date),'[)')
         @> coalesce(new.start_date, current_date);

  if not found then
    raise exception 'No live price for plan % on %', new.fare_plan_id, new.start_date;
  end if;

  new.agreed_price := coalesce(new.agreed_price, v.list_price);

  if new.agreed_price < v.floor_price or new.agreed_price > v.ceiling_price then
    raise exception 'R% is outside the agreed band R%–R%',
      new.agreed_price, v.floor_price, v.ceiling_price;
  end if;

  if new.agreed_price < v.list_price and coalesce(new.discount_reason,'') = '' then
    raise exception 'A discount below list price needs a recorded reason';
  end if;

  new.price_locked_at := now();
  return new;
end; $$ language plpgsql;

What this buys you the day it ships

Revenue splits by plan without anyone tagging anything. A price rise is a new fare_plan_version — existing riders keep their locked rate until you choose to re-rate them, with notice. Corporate bulk rates stop being a spreadsheet. And a seat sold below floor is rejected by the database rather than discovered at month-end.

04End-to-end flows

Every journey through the system

Four customer journeys and the operator journey that carries them. Each step names the system that does the work, so nothing hides behind "and then it's handled".

Diagram key Step in this journey Interchange — another system takes over Terminus — a settled end state
Journey 01

The individual commuter subscription

A person books a standing seat on a route and pays every month. Your highest-volume, highest-value journey.

  1. Enquiry and quote

    Customer picks a route, a boarding stop and a commitment — 5 days or 3, return or one-way. The system lists only the fare plans they qualify for and quotes the list price of each. Nobody types a number.

    fare_plans · fare_plan_versions

  2. Capacity check before anything is promised

    Before a quote is confirmed, the system tests the next 90 days of trips on that route and pattern. If any dated trip cannot seat one more standing rider, the plan is offered as waitlist instead of sale. This is the step that stops you selling a seat you do not have.

    seat_ledger · trips

  3. Price agreed and locked

    Staff may move within the band. Below list demands a reason on the record. Outside the band the database refuses the write. The agreed figure is stamped onto the subscription with a timestamp and never recalculated.

    subscriptions.agreed_price · enforce_fare_band()

  4. Mandate and first payment

    Card or EFT through the gateway for the first period, plus a DebiCheck debit-order mandate for the recurring collection. Subscription stays pending_activation until the first payment clears — never before.

    payments · payment_mandates

  5. Standing reservation materialises

    Activation writes a reservation into the seat ledger for every trip in the paid period matching their pattern — roughly 22 trips for a 5-day month. Those seats are now genuinely unavailable to once-off buyers. This is the mechanism that makes capacity real rather than notional.

    seat_ledger · scheduled job

  6. Riding, day to day

    The rider appears on each trip manifest automatically. The driver marks boarded or no-show on a phone. No-shows accumulate against the account rather than vanishing.

    trip_manifests · bookings

  7. The billing run, every month

    A scheduled job finds subscriptions due, raises a numbered VAT invoice at the locked price, applies any credit balance, presents the debit order, and emails the invoice. Failures land in a worklist for a human — not in silence.

    invoices · billing job · work_items

  8. Change mid-life

    Upgrade, downgrade, pause, change boarding stop. Each is a plan change with a pro-rata credit or charge and a matching adjustment to the seat reservations. Every change is a row in the subscription's history — never an in-place edit.

    subscription_changes · credit_notes

  9. Renewal, or a clean exit

    Renewal is silent while the debit order clears. Cancellation honours the notice period, releases the future seats back to inventory, settles the final balance and records a reason you can report on.

    subscriptions.status · churn_reasons
Journey 02

The company or school account

One organisation pays for many named passengers. Different from Journey 01 in a way the current schema has no room for at all.

  1. The account is a parent, not a customer

    A company record with a billing contact, VAT number, purchase-order requirement and payment terms. Passengers are children of that account — they ride, they never pay. Today's customers table cannot express this relationship.

    accounts · account_members

  2. Bulk quote against a volume plan

    Seat count selects the tier automatically — 10+ or 25+ — and the whole agreement sits inside that plan's band. One negotiated rate, applied to every seat on the contract.

    fare_plans.min_seats

  3. Passenger roster

    Upload or capture the named riders and their boarding stops. Seats are contracted at the account level, so swapping a passenger is an edit to the roster, not a new sale and not a re-price.

    account_members · seat_assignments

  4. Reservations, once, for the whole roster

    The same seat-ledger mechanism as Journey 01, but committed in one block across the contract term. A 25-seat school contract reserves 25 seats on every qualifying trip for the term.

    seat_ledger

  5. One consolidated invoice

    A single monthly tax invoice with a line per passenger or per cost centre, carrying their PO number, on their terms — 30 days from statement rather than on presentation. Not 25 separate invoices.

    invoices · invoice_lines

  6. Reconciliation and dunning

    EFT arrives as one lump against several invoices. The allocation screen splits it. Ageing runs 30/60/90 and the chase sequence escalates from reminder to statement to service hold.

    payment_allocations · ageing view

  7. Term end and renewal

    School terms end on a date, corporate contracts renew. Both produce a usage report — seats contracted against seats actually ridden — which is the number that wins or loses the renewal.

    utilisation report
Journey 03

The once-off rider

Single trip, charter or airport run. Pays upfront, has no subscription, and must never be sold a seat a subscriber already holds.

  1. Search for a trip

    Route and date returns dated trips with genuinely free seats — total capacity minus subscriber reservations minus live holds. Not the loose seats_occupied integer sitting on the row today.

    seat_availability view

  2. Seat held, with a real clock

    Selecting a seat writes a hold with a 15-minute expiry. A scheduled job actually releases expired holds — the current schema has a hold_expiry column that nothing ever reads. Concurrent buyers are serialised by the ledger, so the same seat cannot go twice.

    seat_ledger · hold expiry job

  3. Priced from a once-off plan

    Once-off fares are ordinary fare plans with billing_period = 'once_off' — same bands, same rules. One pricing model covers both halves of the business.

    fare_plans

  4. Paid before confirmed

    Payment clears, the hold converts to a confirmed booking and a numbered invoice plus receipt issue immediately. If payment fails the hold simply lapses and the seat returns to sale.

    payments · invoices · bookings

  5. Ticket, board, complete

    A booking reference with a QR code. The driver scans at the kerb; the rider joins the same manifest as the subscribers. One list, whatever the customer paid.

    trip_manifests

  6. Completed — or refunded

    Completion closes it. Cancellation runs the refund policy by notice window, issues a credit note or gateway refund, and releases the seat far enough ahead that it can be resold.

    credit_notes · refunds
Journey 04

When something goes wrong

Failed collections, cancelled trips, no-shows and disputes. The journey most systems ignore, and the one that decides whether customers stay.

  1. A debit order fails

    The invoice goes overdue and a dunning sequence starts: reminder on day 1, retry on day 3, notice on day 7, service hold on day 14. Every step is logged against the account and the rider keeps their seat until the hold — you do not strand a paying customer over a bank timing issue.

    dunning job · notifications

  2. You cancel a trip

    Breakdown, driver unavailable, weather. Cancelling a trip fans out automatically: notify every rider on the manifest, credit once-off riders in full, and add a pro-rata credit to each subscriber's next invoice. Today that is entirely manual and entirely forgettable.

    trip cancellation · credit_notes

  3. A rider does not show

    Recorded on the manifest, not billable — a subscriber pays for the seat regardless — but tracked, because a pattern of no-shows is your earliest churn signal and a chance to right-size someone from 5 days to 3.

    trip_manifests · churn signals

  4. A billing dispute

    Because the agreed price is locked with a timestamp and every change is a historical row, you can show the customer precisely what was agreed, when, by whom, and what changed since. This is what versioned pricing is really for.

    audit_logs · subscription_changes

  5. Resolved, or lost cleanly

    Credit note, refund or write-off — each a proper accounting document, never an edit to a historical invoice. If they leave, the reason is captured against a fixed list you can actually report on.

    credit_notes · churn_reasons
Journey 05

The operator and the fleet

Not a customer journey, but the one that delivers all four above. You run mixed — owned vehicles plus subcontractors — and those two need different money.

  1. Onboarding and vetting

    A subcontracted operator submits company registration, operating licence, insurance and tax clearance. Each is a document with an expiry date. Nobody gets allocated a trip until their pack is complete and current.

    fleet_owners · documents

  2. Compliance that watches itself

    Driver licences, PrDPs, medicals and vehicle inspections all carry expiry dates. A daily job raises a work item at 60, 30 and 7 days, and blocks allocation on the day it lapses. The columns already exist — nothing currently watches them.

    expiry job · work_items

  3. Timetable generates trips

    Routes gain a schedule — days of week, departure times, seasonal exceptions. A job materialises dated trips 90 days ahead so subscriber seats have somewhere to be reserved. Trips stop being hand-created rows.

    route_schedules · trip generation job

  4. Allocation against demand

    Each trip already knows its committed seats from the ledger. Allocation picks a vehicle whose capacity covers it and a driver who is compliant and not double-booked, preferring owned fleet before subcontractors — cheapest capacity first.

    seat_ledger · allocation rules

  5. Two kinds of cost

    An owned trip accrues internal cost — fuel, driver hours, maintenance. A subcontracted trip accrues a payable to the operator at their agreed rate. Both hang off the same trip, which is the only way you see true margin per route.

    trip_costs · operator_rates

  6. Operator payout

    Month-end totals completed trips per operator, applies commission, deducts penalties for no-shows or cancellations, and produces a remittance they can check against their own records.

    operator_statements · payouts
